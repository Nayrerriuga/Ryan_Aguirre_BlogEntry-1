Blog #1: Reflection On the First Meeting for Advanced Technology and Project Management

September 13, 2025 • IT Building • 10:00 AM (PST)

It was a truly lovely day, a bright Saturday morning on September 13, 2025. The sun eased into the sky as if the week itself was offering a gentle reward: an opportunity to pause, to gather, and to learn. At 10:00 AM Philippine Time, the IT building hummed with that particular blend of quiet activity and expectant chatter that always makes me feel welcome in an academic space. The atmosphere was lively yet purposeful, the perfect environment for a Project Management class designed to sharpen skills that we, as professionals and future leaders, will use time and again.

I remember walking into the dedicated classroom and feeling that familiar mixture of curiosity and resolve. It wasn’t my first time attending face-to-face sessions, but it felt like a new rhythm after years of hybrid and online learning. There was something immediate and tangible about being physically present that I had missed: the cadence of conversation, the unspoken cues of engagement, the brief side smiles when a joke landed. Our facilitator—calm, professional, and approachable—stood at the front, and within minutes I realized why this particular session felt different. The way they held the class, the clarity of their voice, and the subtle warmth in their demeanor reminded me of the mentor who had shaped my own professional path. I could see the personality of my own mentor in our facilitator; it was a small revelation but a powerful one, because it set the tone for the entire day.

We started with introductions, a purposeful exercise designed to reveal the individuals in the group, not just their functions. Each participant briefly shared background details: their work, studies, reason for enrolling, and expectations for the course. Every introduction opened a glimpse into one’s journey: a systems analyst seeking stronger project leadership, a recent graduate eager to absorb proven practices, and a mid-career professional hoping to formalize skills learned informally. My own sharing was direct and clear. I discussed my current role, my pursuit of a Master’s in Information Technology, and my hope that this course would enhance both my applied abilities and theoretical grounding.

These introductions did more than just fill out a roll call sheet. They made it easier for us to perceive each other as partners in a same goal. That brief exercise immediately generated energy in the form of laughter, recognition nods, and the kind of human connection that is conducive to learning. It got me thinking about how crucial trust is to teamwork, which is what project management aims to foster. A project thrives on the relationships between individuals who are prepared to talk, make concessions, and contribute rather than only on a timeline or budget.

The Pre-class Survey: A Mirror to Our Learning Attitudes

Following introductions, our facilitator steered the conversation to a survey we had completed before the class in August. The survey was designed to capture our self-perceptions about learning, technology adoption, collaboration, and engagement. Questions ranged from “I am confident in my ability to adopt new technological tools” to “I learn best through hands-on projects,” each rated on a scale of 1 to 5.

There was something inherently reflective about discussing the survey results together. The facilitator used the aggregated responses not to judge, but to understand us—to see, in statistical form, the strengths and areas where we could grow. It was insightful to hear how varied our attitudes were: some of us rated ourselves high on leveraging technology and collaborating across teams; others admitted to being more comfortable with solitary study and incremental learning. For me, the exercise felt instructive and validating. It confirmed that while we carried different histories and preferences, we shared a common willingness to learn, a key ingredient in any successful project team.

I also appreciated the facilitator’s approach: rather than lecturing about the virtues of collaboration, he invited us to see the survey as diagnostic. That gentle, learner-centered guidance reinforced an important truth—that teaching is rarely about delivering answers and more about revealing questions. Such an approach resonated with my own experience as a self-directed learner: when an instructor creates space for reflection, students are more likely to internalize lessons and adapt them to their own contexts. This approach was a familiar one; this was the approach once used by my academic mentor which is really close to the heart of mine.

Course Requirements in Context: PG05–PG09

Once we moved beyond the introductions and the survey, the facilitator outlined the course deliverables and how it is mapped to the PG05, PG06, PG07, and PG09 modules found in our syllabus. These requirements are not arbitrary; they are carefully designed to ensure both individual growth and collaborative competence.

PG05 challenges us to discover new tools and techniques for managing projects. Whether the recommendation is MS Project, OpenProject, or the humble Excel spreadsheet, the learning outcome is the same: we learn to track goals, manage schedules, and visualize dependencies. As a professional who has used a variety of tools at work, I welcomed this outcome. PG06 emphasizes team-driven development of a solution to a specific problem. This is where lived teamwork becomes crucial; the idea is to practice coordination, role distribution, and conflict resolution.

PG07 focuses on communication: presenting results clearly and persuasively. Many technically strong teams fail not because of poor engineering but because of poor communication. Finally, PG09 measures our individual capacity to conduct computing research. The capstone project, which needs to align with the institution’s Vision—to be a premiere research university—is an opportunity to connect academic aims with real-world impact.

Deep Dive: PMBOK 6th vs 7th Edition

A major portion of our discussion centered on the PMBOK guide. I found myself comparing the 7th edition with the 6th edition to better appreciate the evolution of the standard. The 6th edition emphasized knowledge areas and lifecycle phases: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring, and closing. It organized project management around clearly defined domains—scope, schedule, cost, and quality.

The 7th edition, however, felt more like a mindset shift. Instead of a rigid process map, it introduced the System for Value Delivery. The focus moved from prescriptive processes to delivering value, exercising leadership, and understanding systems. Where the 6th edition taught us what steps to take, the 7th invited us to think about why and how those steps deliver value.

"For me, this evolution aligned with my professional instincts. Projects are not just boxes to tick; they are complex socio-technical endeavors that require sensitivity to stakeholders."

Teamwork and Thinking Outside the Box

One of the themes that kept recurring was teamwork. Our facilitator emphasized that project success is rarely a solitary achievement. Interestingly, the facilitator also acknowledged that they would not teach the minutiae of every tool. Instead, they urged us to be professionals who can self-learn. That resonated deeply with me. Much of my own growth has come from self-study: seeking resources, experimenting with tools, and learning by doing.

Before the session ended, we were given a deceptively simple task: connect the four corners of a square without lifting the pen. When the facilitator revealed the trick—that the solution requires drawing a line outside the box—a collective moment of surprise filled the room. That exercise was a metaphor for creative problem solving. It reinforced the idea that problem solving in project management often benefits from unconventional approaches.

Closing Thoughts and Personal Commitments

I left class feeling energized. I’ve resolved to approach the capstone with intentional alignment to the institution’s mission, deepen my tool proficiency through self-study, and practice intentional team-building habits.

This Saturday in September felt like a concentrated lesson in what project management truly is: a human practice that combines method, judgment, and interpersonal skill. I am grateful for the face-to-face encounter and the rekindled appreciation for in-person collaboration. I will meet the challenges of this course with curiosity and discipline.

— Ryan A. Aguirre
