A SaturdayReflections on the Path to Data Protection

By Ryan Aguirre • March 14, 2026

It was a humid Saturday morning, March 14, 2026, when I settled into my seat for our session on Data Privacy and Protection Management. As a Computer Science Instructor at NEMSU, I usually spend my time on the other side of the lectern, breaking down complex logic and teaching my students how to build systems from the ground up. But that morning, I was a student again, and the atmosphere felt different—heavier, more consequential. The air in the room seemed to carry the weight of the digital world we are currently building. The facilitator began with a framework that immediately shifted my perspective: the DOST 6P’s. I realized that as I work on my own research involving OCR and records management for the Lianga government, I hadn’t been looking at the "Policies" or "Patents" enough. We weren't just there to learn how to code; we were there to learn how to quantify the impact of our science against the public funds that fuel it. I thought about the three semesters I’ve spent teaching at NEMSU, and how often I focus on the "Product" without considering the "Policy" that ensures that product survives in a regulated world.

The discussion moved into a mantra that I’ve since started repeating to myself: Think, Reflect, and Write. The facilitator challenged us to ask the questions no one else has asked. For me, that meant looking at my students and my own master’s capstone through a lens of extreme rigor. It’s not enough to just "do" research; it must be systematic, verifiable, and empirical. I found myself reflecting on the sampling methods I teach. If the gap between our data and the reality of the population is too wide, we aren't just failing an assignment—we are misrepresenting the people whose lives are embedded in that data. This was the first spark of a realization that I was no longer just a "developer," but a budding guardian of information. I began to visualize my current research on fishermen monitoring systems using the Haversine formula. If my data collection is flawed, the safety of those fishermen is compromised. The ethics of data are not separate from the logic of the code; they are the soul of it.

As the morning progressed into the technicalities of Republic Act 10173, the room felt smaller, the responsibility more tangible. In my Information Systems classes, I’ve always made it a point to discuss the Data Privacy Act of 2012, but the facilitator’s deep dive into the distinction between raw "data" and "information" hit home in a way it never had before. Section 3, paragraph (c) defines the Data Subject as the individual at the heart of the processing. It’s a sobering thought: every string of text in a database represents a person’s right to privacy. I started thinking about the student records I manage and the municipal systems I am designing. Each entry is a heartbeat, a life, a story that deserves protection. We then pivoted to physical security, something we software-focused professionals often take for granted. I thought of the data centers I’ve visited, the strict 30-day access rules for backbone IT professionals, and the "onion-like" layers of protection required by Chapter V, Section 20. It isn’t just about firewalls; it’s about the security guards, the locks, and the physical barriers that protect the servers from unauthorized hands. As a CS instructor, I realized I had been doing a disservice by not emphasizing the "physical" in "physical security" enough. A master hacker doesn't need to bypass a firewall if they can just walk through an unlocked door.

"I sat there, staring at the provision for Chapter VIII, realizing that the code I write today could literally be the reason someone faces a two-million-peso fine tomorrow. It changed my stand point from being an enthusiast to being a professional with a heavy mantle of responsibility."

One specific moment in the discussion truly overwhelmed me: the scenarios of compromise. The facilitator described a situation where privacy is compromised but security is maintained—the classic social engineering trap. I pictured an office Christmas party where an address list is shared without restrictions. Even with military-grade encryption on the servers, the human element failed. It reinforced my standing point that education is the ultimate firewall. If we don't teach the "People" in our systems how to value privacy, the "Hardware" and "Software" are useless. Then came the darker scenario: the total collapse of both privacy and security. I was transported back to my early days as an IT professional, watching worms spread through a network with lightning speed, where the only "fix" was the nuclear option of reformatting every drive. I remembered the sweat on my brow back then, the panic of seeing every desktop on the organization turn into a brick. Today, we have IPS signatures and Zero Trust Network Access, but the threat remains just as visceral. I started to see the role of a Data Protection Officer (DPO) as a sort of digital doctor, constantly monitoring the health of an organization and preparing for the inevitable "virus."

We spent hours dissecting the Data Privacy Manual. It’s a document I used to think was just "HR paperwork," but the facilitator showed us it is actually the organization's bible for compliance. It bridges the gap between the National Privacy Commission’s issuances and the daily operations of a clerk or a programmer. I thought about NEMSU and the Lianga Municipal Government. Do they have a manual that is actually lived, or just one that sits on a shelf? I felt a surge of interest in practicing this field. Being a DPO means you are the bridge. You have to speak "Law" to the lawyers and "Code" to the programmers. It is a multi-disciplinary challenge that fits my background in Computer Science and my current pursuit of a Master’s in IT. I started taking feverish notes, not just for the exam, but for my future. I saw how Section 11, 12, and 13 of the Act provide the skeleton for how we should handle sensitive personal information. It’s a recipe for trust. If you follow it, you build a fortress of credibility; if you ignore it, you build a house of cards.

The facilitator then discussed the concept of "Data as the New Oil." This is a phrase we hear often in tech circles, but the nuance provided during the session was critical: oil is only valuable if it is refined. Unrefined data is just a mess; lost data is a catastrophe. I reflected on the research study from December 2025 by Malatyinszki Szilard. Data only becomes a resource when it is embedded into decision-making. As I develop my web-based records management system, I have to ensure that the OCR-extracted data isn't just stored—it must be analyzed securely to provide value to the municipal leaders without compromising the citizens' rights. This balance is the true art of modern information management. I realized that my role as an instructor is to teach my students how to be "refiners," not just "collectors." We have enough collectors in the world; what we need are people who can handle the power of data with surgical precision.

By the time we reached the discussion on penalties, a hush fell over the room. Hearing that improper disposal under Section 27 could lead to two years in prison, and intentional breaches under Section 29 could mean three years and a two-million-peso fine, made the stakes crystal clear. This isn't just "tech"; this is legal and ethical warfare. I thought about the old hard drives I’ve seen lying around in various offices, likely filled with unencrypted personal data. Under the law, those are ticking time bombs. As an instructor, I felt a renewed urgency to make RA 10173 a central theme of my Information Systems subject. My students need to know that their career could be over before it starts if they don't respect these provisions. I found myself looking forward to the field of Data Protection more than ever. It offers a level of professional responsibility that goes beyond the "cool factor" of a new framework or a sleek UI. It’s about protecting the society I live in.

The closing moments of the session were dedicated to the USEP Official Documents Archiving System (UDAS). I tried to access the page on my laptop, watching as the departments appeared but the folders remained grayed out, locked behind a wall of "Need-to-Know" access. It was the perfect visual representation of everything we had discussed. It wasn't about hiding information; it was about ensuring that the right people had the right access at the right time. It was a functioning example of Section 11, paragraph (d)—data that is adequate and not excessive. I left the room that day feeling a mix of weight and excitement. I’m no longer just looking at a career in development or teaching; I’m looking forward to the field of a Data Protection Officer. My standpoint has shifted: I am an educator, a researcher, and now, a protector of the digital frontier. The journey of studying this field has only just begun, and I am ready to dive deeper into the provisions and protections that will define the next decade of my career.

As I drove home, the concepts of Zero Trust and IPS signatures swirled in my head alongside the legal clauses of Chapter VIII. I realized that my perspective as a CS Instructor gives me a unique advantage in this field. I can see the vulnerabilities in the code that a lawyer might miss, and I can see the legal risks in the system architecture that a programmer might ignore. This session was overwhelming, yes, but it was the kind of "overwhelmed" that precedes growth. It was the feeling of a horizon expanding. I thought about my students at NEMSU—the next generation of IT professionals—and I knew exactly what my first lecture on Monday would be. We aren't just building apps anymore; we are building a safer, more private future for our community. And that is a story worth telling, a research worth pursuing, and a law worth protecting.

“We are no longer just building systems; we are building trust.”

Reflecting back on that day, I am struck by how much my personal standpoint has evolved. I am no longer just a contract instructor filling a role; I am a professional on a mission to integrate security into every line of code I teach. The journey toward becoming a DPO is daunting, filled with countless provisions and ever-changing threats, but it is a journey I am eager to take. Whether I am troubleshooting a piece of logic or drafting a privacy manual, the goal remains the same: to protect the individual. As I look at the grayed-out folders of UDAS or the encrypted strings in my own projects, I don't see barriers—I see the shields that keep our digital society safe. This is the future I am looking forward to, and it started on that humid Saturday morning in March.
