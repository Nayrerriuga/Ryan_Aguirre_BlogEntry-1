Reflection On the First Meeting for the Advanced Technology and Project Management

By Ryan A. Aguirre • September 13, 2025 • IT Building • 10:00 AM (Philippine Time)

It was a truly lovely day, a bright Saturday morning on September 13, 2025. The sun eased into the sky as if the week itself was offering a gentle reward: an opportunity to pause, to gather, and to learn. At 10:00 AM Philippine Time, the IT building hummed with that particular blend of quiet activity and expectant chatter that always makes me feel welcome in an academic space. The atmosphere was lively yet purposeful, the perfect environment for a Project Management class designed to sharpen skills that we, as professionals and future leaders, will use time and again.

I remember walking into the dedicated classroom and feeling that familiar mixture of curiosity and resolve. It wasn’t my first time attending face-to-face sessions, but it felt like a new rhythm after years of hybrid and online learning. There was something immediate and tangible about being physically present that I had missed: the cadence of conversation, the unspoken cues of engagement, the brief side smiles when a joke landed. Our facilitator. Calm, professional, and approachable stood at the front, and within minutes I realized why this particular session felt different. The way they held the class, the clarity of their voice, and the subtle warmth in their demeanor reminded me of the mentor who had shaped my own professional path. I could see the personality of my own mentor in our facilitator; it was a small revelation but a powerful one, because it set the tone for the entire day.

We started with introductions, a purposeful exercise designed to reveal the individuals in the group, not just their functions. Each participant briefly shared background details: their work, studies, reason for enrolling, and expectations for the course. Every introduction opened a glimpse into one’s journey: a systems analyst seeking stronger project leadership, a recent graduate eager to absorb proven practices, and a mid-career professional hoping to formalize skills learned informally. My own sharing was direct and clear. I discussed my current role, my pursuit of a Master’s in Information Technology, and my hope that this course would enhance both my applied abilities and theoretical grounding.

These introductions did more than just fill out a roll call sheet. They made it easier for us to perceive each other as partners in a same goal. That brief exercise immediately generated energy in the form of laughter, recognition nods, and the kind of human connection that is conducive to learning. It got me thinking about how crucial trust is to teamwork, which is what project management aims to foster. A project thrives on the relationships between individuals who are prepared to talk, make concessions, and contribute rather than only on a timeline or budget.

The Pre-class Survey: A Mirror to Our Learning Attitudes

Following introductions, our facilitator steered the conversation to a survey we had completed before the class in August. The survey was designed to capture our self-perceptions about learning, technology adoption, collaboration, and engagement. Questions ranged from “I am confident in my ability to adopt new technological tools” to “I learn best through hands-on projects,” each rated on a scale of 1 to 5.

There was something inherently reflective about discussing the survey results together. The facilitator used the aggregated responses not to judge, but to understand us to see, in statistical form, the strengths and areas where we could grow. It was insightful to hear how varied our attitudes were: some of us rated ourselves high on leveraging technology and collaborating across teams; others admitted to being more comfortable with solitary study and incremental learning. For me, the exercise felt instructive and validating. It confirmed that while we carried different histories and preferences, we shared a common willingness to learn, a key ingredient in any successful project team.

I also appreciated the facilitator’s approach: rather than lecturing about the virtues of collaboration, he invited us to see the survey as diagnostic. That gentle, learner-centered guidance reinforced an important truth, that teaching is rarely about delivering answers and more about revealing questions. Such an approach resonated with my own experience as a self-directed learner: when an instructor creates space for reflection, students are more likely to internalize lessons and adapt them to their own contexts. This approach was a familiar one, this was the approach that was once used by my academic mentor which is really close to the heart of mine.

Course Requirements in Context: PG05–PG09

Once we moved beyond the introductions and the survey, the facilitator outlined the course deliverables and how it is mapped to the PG05, PG06, PG07, and PG09 modules found in our syllabus. These requirements are not arbitrary; they are carefully designed to ensure both individual growth and collaborative competence.

PG05 challenges us to discover new tools and techniques for managing projects. Whether the recommendation is MS Project, OpenProject, or the humble Excel spreadsheet, the learning outcome is the same: we learn to track goals, manage schedules, and visualize dependencies. As a professional who has used a variety of tools at work, I welcomed this outcome. The module allows for flexibility in tools while stressing the importance of their effective application.

PG06 emphasizes team-driven development of a solution to a specific problem. This is where lived teamwork becomes crucial; the idea is to practice coordination, role distribution, and conflict resolution. The team-based Industry Analysis project will allow us to understand how disparate skills come together to solve real problems.

PG07 focuses on communication: presenting results clearly and persuasively in class and in public forums. Many technically strong teams fail not because of poor engineering but because of poor communication. This module elevates the soft skills essential to project success.

Finally, PG09 measures our individual capacity to conduct computing research. This is where critical thinking, literature review, and rigor are put on display. The capstone project, which needs to align with the institution’s Vision — to be a premiere research university — and Mission — which emphasizes continuous development, research, and community service — is an opportunity to connect academic aims with real-world impact.

I felt a strong sense of duty hearing these requirements. This was not mere academic checkboxing; the syllabus, approved at the institutional level, was a formal recognition that our work must connect with larger goals: to improve community outcomes, contribute to research, and build capabilities that make us globally competitive. The gravity of that expectation inspired me to approach the course with commitment and intentionality.

Deep Dive: PMBOK 6th vs 7th Edition

A major portion of our discussion centered on the PMBOK guide. I downloaded the 7th edition, as instructed, but I found myself comparing it with the 6th edition to better appreciate the evolution of the standard.

The 6th edition emphasized knowledge areas and the project lifecycle phases: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring and controlling, and closing. It organized project management around clearly defined domains — scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, communications, risk, procurement, and stakeholder management. That structure is valuable because it offers a roadmap for managers: discrete areas you can study and apply.

The 7th edition, however, felt more like a mindset shift. Instead of a rigid process map, it introduced the System for Value Delivery and Project Performance Domains. The focus moved from prescriptive processes to delivering value, exercising leadership, understanding systems, and tailoring approaches to context. Concepts like stewardship, adaptability, and systems thinking became integral to the framework. Where the 6th edition taught us what steps to take, the 7th invited us to think about why and how those steps deliver value.

For me, this evolution aligned with my professional instincts. Projects are not just boxes to tick; they are complex socio-technical endeavors that require sensitivity to stakeholders, adaptability to changing conditions, and a focus on outcomes rather than outputs. The 7th edition’s inclusion of models, methods, and artifacts gave practical tools while reminding us that no one-size-fits-all process can replace professional judgment.

Vision, Mission, and the Capstone Imperative

Our facilitator also connected course expectations with the institution’s Vision and Mission: continuous development, research, community service, and resource stewardship. The program aspires to be a premiere research university, and our capstone project must align with those aspirations. That linkage is important because it anchors our projects in a broader institutional purpose. When I think about the capstone, I no longer see it as a personal milestone only; I see it as a contribution to a larger body of knowledge and service.

That framing influenced how I plan to approach the capstone. Rather than choosing an easy or convenient topic, I felt compelled to choose a problem that matters — that can deliver measurable value to our community, whether in education, local governance, or small-business support. The facilitator’s emphasis on alignment with institutional goals made the project feel purposeful and consequential.

Teamwork, Professionalism, and Self-directed Learning

One of the themes that kept recurring in the discussion was teamwork. Our facilitator emphasized, and I wholeheartedly agree, that project success is rarely a solitary achievement. It requires clear communication, defined roles, mutual trust, and the humility to accept feedback. To cultivate teamwork, we were encouraged to practice intentional leadership, delegate responsibilities, and use collaborative tools, from MS Project and OpenProject to Excel and ServiceNow for Agile workflows.

Interestingly, the facilitator also acknowledged that they would not teach the minutiae of every tool. Instead, they urged us to be professionals who can self-learn. That resonated deeply with me. Much of my own growth has come from self-study: seeking resources, experimenting with tools, and learning by doing. The facilitator’s stance — that we should be proactive in acquiring practical skills, felt empowering rather than intimidating. It reminded me that formal instruction is a scaffold; the lifelong work of mastering tools and techniques rests on our willingness to explore independently.

To prepare myself, I resolved to balance guided learning with self-study. I will take advantage of the course’s structure to practice project management principles and use outside resources to gain tool-specific competencies. This hybrid approach, combining instructor-led insights with personal initiative, seems like the most pragmatic and effective path to professional mastery. This could really help me in terms of improving my project management skills, for an individual like me that usually work on a project solely.

Think Outside the Box: A Simple Puzzle, A Big Lesson

Before the session ended, we were given a deceptively simple task: connect the four corners of a square without lifting the pen and without retracing lines. I struggled with it at first, like many of my classmates. When the facilitator revealed the trick that the solution requires drawing a line outside the box, a collective moment of surprise and laughter filled the room.

That exercise was more than a game. It was a metaphor for creative problem solving. Many times we limit ourselves by the implicit boundaries of conventional thinking. By encouraging us to “think outside the box,” the facilitator offered a powerful nudge: consider adjacent possibilities, challenge assumptions, and look for solutions beyond the immediately visible constraints.

That small, bright moment became a theme for me. It reinforced the idea that problem solving in project management, whether designing a schedule, resolving a stakeholder conflict, or optimizing resources, often benefits from unconventional approaches. Creativity and relevance are not opposites; they are complementary strategies for producing better outcomes.

Reflections on Face-to-Face Learning

I left class feeling energized and thoughtful. In-person academic engagement is highly preferable, and i found the format unexpectedly invigorating. Face-to-face learning offered immediacy: feedback came in real time, discussions flowed without the delay of digital back-and-forth, and the social rhythms of the classroom fostered a deeper level of connection.

For certain subjects, project management being one of them I believe the absorption of ideas and the development of interpersonal skills are more effective in person. Practicing leadership, negotiating roles, and presenting work to a live audience are skills that thrive on human interaction. While online and hybrid modalities have their strengths, there is a kind of kinetic learning that only happens when people share the same physical space.

Personal Commitments and Next Steps

After the class, I made a few quiet commitments to myself. First, I would approach the capstone with intentional alignment to the institution’s mission, choosing a project with tangible community or research impact. Second, I would deepen my tool proficiency through deliberate self-study, focusing on one or two project management tools and integrating them into my workflow. Third, I would practice team-building habits: clear role definitions, scheduled check-ins, and open channels for feedback.

Finally, I resolved to carry forward the spirit of the puzzle: to deliberately seek perspectives beyond the obvious, to question assumptions, and to prioritize creativity in problem solving. These commitments felt practical and grounded — steps I could take immediately to improve both my learning experience and my contribution to any project team.

Closing Thoughts

That Saturday in September,the sunlight in the IT building, the careful facilitation, the conversations, the survey, the PMBOK discussion, and the little puzzle felt like a concentrated lesson in what project management truly is: a human practice that combines method, judgment, and interpersonal skill. I walked away moved by the facilitator’s ability to capture our attention and guide us gently yet purposefully. Seeing my mentor’s personality reflected in them was both comforting and inspiring: it reminded me that thoughtful teaching can be both rigorous and warm.

I am grateful for the face-to-face encounter. After a long spell of remote learning, this session rekindled my appreciation for in-person collaboration and the kind of learning that emerges from direct human exchange. As I close this reflection, I am mindful that learning is iterative. The course will present challenges and deadlines, questions and discoveries, and I will meet them with curiosity and discipline.

This is the end of my first blog entry for the Advanced Technology and Project Management course. I hope that reading this account gives you a small window into the experience of sitting in that bright classroom on September 13, 2025. May we all find mentors who ignite our curiosity, peers who push us to grow, and puzzles that teach us to look beyond the box. Wishing everyone a productive and joyful learning journey.

— Ryan A. Aguirre
